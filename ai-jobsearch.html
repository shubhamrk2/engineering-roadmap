Job search method.

A job search is a pipeline you can measure, not a mood. This sheet gives you the method: how to read a posting, match it to evidence you can link to, check that it is real and open to you, apply as soon as the evidence is credible, track what happens, and compare offers on a written matrix. It is a method, not market data: it names no salaries, no vacancies and no hiring numbers, because those go stale and you must research them yourself, on the day.

The search as a pipeline with evidence at every stage

Postings go in at the top, offers come out at the bottom, and most of what you start with leaks away on the way. That is normal. The method is about spending effort where the evidence is strongest and measuring what actually happens.

1
Save narrowly. A saved posting should map to one of the role families in the next section. If it does not, it is noise, however attractive the company is.
2
Verify before you invest. Stage 2 is where closed postings, duplicate reposts, scams and “remote, but not from India” roles are removed. It costs ten minutes and saves hours.
3
Apply on credible evidence, not on completeness. The plan sets Gate 3 for the first real applications once Project 1 is public. Waiting to finish every topic first is the most common way to lose a month.
4
Evidence is the currency. A link to a repo, a README with honest limitations and a demo transcript beats an adjective on a résumé.
5
Leaks are data. If many applications get silence, the problem is usually at stage 2 or 3 (targeting or résumé). If screens happen but interviews do not follow, it is the screen. Each stage has a different fix, and only a log tells you which.
Red pencil — two rules that cannot bend

Never misrepresent your experience. Do not claim Python or AI depth you do not have, do not inflate a title or a duration, and do not list a tool you cannot discuss for five minutes. Never put confidential employer information anywhere: not on a résumé, not in a repo, not in a portfolio, not in an interview answer. Both rules protect you, because the first one is tested live and the second one has contractual consequences. Sheet 23 (Python from zero) shows what a defensible, honest project looks like.

Reading a job description

A posting is a wish list written by several people. Your job is to separate what is required from what is hoped for, and to notice what is missing.

Red flags

  • An unrealistic stack. Twenty tools, three cloud providers, two languages and “5+ years of GenAI” for a role at a young company is either a copy-paste wish list or a sign that nobody has scoped the job. Years of experience asked for a technology cannot exceed how long it has existed.
  • Vague scope. No product, no team, no responsibilities beyond “work on cutting-edge AI”. Ask what the first project would be; a real team can answer.
  • Unpaid work. A large, unpaid assignment that looks like the company’s real backlog, or a “trial period” without pay. Use the take-home policy below.
  • Money going the wrong way. Any fee for applying, training, a laptop, or a “security deposit” is a scam pattern, not a hiring practice.
  • Pressure and secrecy. “Respond in 24 hours or we move on”, no named hiring manager, contact only through a messaging app.
  • A salary that is strikingly out of line with similar postings you have researched, with no explanation. Too generous can be as suspicious as too low.
Interviewers ask

“You do not meet the years of Python they asked for. Why apply?” Because the posting’s responsibilities match work I have done (integration, APIs, retries, data mapping) and I can show it. I have X months of focused Python, here are the repos, and here is the gap I am closing and by when. I am not claiming more than that. Follow-up they will ask: “what would you struggle with in the first month?” Name a real item and your plan for it.

A keyword-overlap helper (a crude aid, not a verdict)

Keep a plain evidence file: one line per skill, term | link you can open. The helper finds which terms from your vocabulary list appear in a posting and sorts them into matched (you listed a link), unevidenced (you listed the term but no link) and gaps (not in your file at all). It matches whole terms only, so rag does not fire inside storage.

Be honest about what this does. It counts words. It cannot tell “required” from “nice to have”, cannot judge how deep your evidence is, and misses any skill that is not in your vocabulary list. A high percentage is not permission to apply and a low one is not a reason to skip. Use it to find the gaps you would otherwise miss, then read the posting yourself.

keyword_match.py — you must be able to explain the whole-term regex
"""Crude keyword overlap between a job posting and your evidence file.

A keyword overlap is an aid for noticing gaps. It is not a verdict on whether you
should apply: it cannot read context, seniority or how deep your evidence goes.
"""
import re


def find_terms(text, vocab):
    """Return the sorted list of vocabulary terms that appear in text.

    Matching is case-insensitive and whole-term: 'rag' must not match 'storage',
    and 'c++' must be found next to punctuation. Terms may contain spaces
    ('vector database'). Duplicates in vocab are reported once.
    """
    lowered = text.lower()
    found = set()
    for term in vocab:
        t = term.strip().lower()
        if not t:
            continue
        pattern = r"(?<![a-z0-9+#])" + re.escape(t) + r"(?![a-z0-9+#])"
        if re.search(pattern, lowered):
            found.add(t)
    return sorted(found)


def parse_evidence(text):
    """Parse 'term | link' lines into {term: link}.

    Blank lines and lines starting with '#' are ignored. Terms are lower-cased
    and stripped. A line with no '|' (or an empty link) keeps the term with the
    link '' : a claim without evidence.
    """
    out = {}
    for raw in text.splitlines():
        line = raw.strip()
        if not line or line.startswith("#"):
            continue
        term, _, link = line.partition("|")
        term = term.strip().lower()
        if term:
            out[term] = link.strip()
    return out


def match_report(posting, evidence, vocab):
    """Compare the posting's vocabulary terms with an evidence dict.

    Returns a dict with:
      terms       sorted terms found in the posting
      matched     {term: link} for terms with a non-empty evidence link
      unevidenced sorted terms you listed but with no link (do not count as matched)
      gaps        sorted terms in the posting that are not in your evidence at all
      coverage    len(matched) / len(terms), rounded to 2 places; 0.0 if no terms
    """
    terms = find_terms(posting, vocab)
    matched, unevidenced, gaps = {}, [], []
    for t in terms:
        if t in evidence and evidence[t]:
            matched[t] = evidence[t]
        elif t in evidence:
            unevidenced.append(t)
        else:
            gaps.append(t)
    coverage = round(len(matched) / len(terms), 2) if terms else 0.0
    return {"terms": terms, "matched": matched, "unevidenced": sorted(unevidenced),
            "gaps": sorted(gaps), "coverage": coverage}

You must be able to explain this line

(?<![a-z0-9+#])term(?![a-z0-9+#]) is a lookbehind and a lookahead. They assert that the characters either side of the term are not letters, digits, + or #, without consuming them. That is why c++ is found in “some C++, some CI/CD” but c++17 and cpython do not match c++ or python. A plain \b would fail on c++ because + is not a word character.

Predict: the posting says “experience with Kubernetes preferred” and your evidence file has no kubernetes line. Where does it appear, and does it change the coverage?

It appears under gaps, and it lowers coverage like any other term, because the helper does not know “preferred” from “required”. That is the tool’s main weakness: you must weigh the gaps yourself. One preferred gap and one required gap are very different, and a percentage hides that.

Red pencil — the link-less line

Adding kubernetes to your evidence file with no link feels harmless. The helper puts it under unevidenced on purpose, as a reminder that it is a claim, not evidence. If you cannot paste a link that proves it, either build the proof or leave the line off the résumé. Never let the helper’s output nudge you into adding a skill you have not used.

One CSV file, ten columns, on a personal device. It is the only way to answer “what is actually happening?” with a number instead of a feeling.

The script uses the standard csv module, which handles commas and quotes inside a field for you. It refuses an unknown column or a status outside the list, because a typo like aplied would otherwise silently drop a row out of every count.

posting_log.py
"""A plain CSV log of the postings you track, with a summary and follow-up dates."""
import csv
import os
from datetime import date, timedelta

COLUMNS = ["date_added", "company", "role", "source", "url", "status",
           "fit", "applied_on", "follow_up_on", "notes"]
STATUSES = ["saved", "applied", "screen", "interview", "take-home",
            "offer", "rejected", "withdrawn", "ghosted"]
RESPONDED = {"screen", "interview", "take-home", "offer", "rejected"}


def add_row(path, row):
    """Append one posting to the CSV at path, writing the header if the file is new.

    Missing columns are written as ''. An unknown column name or a status that is
    not in STATUSES raises ValueError (and nothing is written). A missing or empty
    status is stored as 'saved'.
    """
    unknown = set(row) - set(COLUMNS)
    if unknown:
        raise ValueError("unknown columns: " + ", ".join(sorted(unknown)))
    status = row.get("status") or "saved"
    if status not in STATUSES:
        raise ValueError("bad status: %r" % status)
    full = {c: row.get(c, "") for c in COLUMNS}
    full["status"] = status
    new_file = not os.path.exists(path) or os.path.getsize(path) == 0
    with open(path, "a", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        w = csv.DictWriter(f, fieldnames=COLUMNS)
        if new_file:
            w.writeheader()
        w.writerow(full)


def read_rows(path):
    """Return the rows of the CSV as a list of dicts; [] if the file does not exist."""
    if not os.path.exists(path):
        return []
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        return list(csv.DictReader(f))


def next_follow_up(applied_on, days=7):
    """Return the follow-up date: applied_on plus days, moved to Monday if it lands on a weekend."""
    d = applied_on + timedelta(days=days)
    while d.weekday() >= 5:
        d += timedelta(days=1)
    return d


def summarise(rows):
    """Count rows per status.

    Returns a dict with every status in STATUSES order (zero when absent) plus
    'total'. A status outside STATUSES is counted under 'other' (only present
    when there is at least one).
    """
    counts = {s: 0 for s in STATUSES}
    other = 0
    for r in rows:
        s = r.get("status", "")
        if s in counts:
            counts[s] += 1
        else:
            other += 1
    if other:
        counts["other"] = other
    counts["total"] = len(rows)
    return counts


def due_follow_ups(rows, today):
    """Return [(company, role)] for rows still 'applied' or 'screen' whose follow_up_on is today or earlier.

    Rows with an empty or unparseable follow_up_on are skipped. Sorted by
    follow_up_on, then company.
    """
    due = []
    for r in rows:
        if r.get("status") not in ("applied", "screen"):
            continue
        try:
            when = date.fromisoformat(r.get("follow_up_on", ""))
        except ValueError:
            continue
        if when <= today:
            due.append((when, r.get("company", ""), r.get("role", "")))
    due.sort()
    return [(c, ro) for _, c, ro in due]


def response_rate(rows):
    """Share of applied postings that got any human response.

    Only rows with a non-empty applied_on count. Responded statuses: screen,
    interview, take-home, offer, rejected. Returns a float rounded to 2 places,
    or None when nothing has been applied to.
    """
    applied = [r for r in rows if r.get("applied_on")]
    if not applied:
        return None
    got = sum(1 for r in applied if r.get("status") in RESPONDED)
    return round(got / len(applied), 2)

Ranking roles: fit, compensation signal, readiness

You cannot apply to everything and you should not apply to whatever is newest. A transparent weighted score makes you write down what you care about, then lets the arithmetic argue with your mood.

The rubric

CriterionRate 1 to 5 by askingExample weight (you set yours)
fitHow much of the responsibilities can you evidence today? 1 = almost none, 5 = most.3
payCompensation signal against the range you researched. 1 = below your floor or unknown, 5 = at or above your target. Advertised ranges only; unknown is a 2 or 3, not a 5.1
readyInterview readiness: could you pass their likely process now? 1 = no, 5 = yes.2
growthWill you learn the thing you want: Python depth, production AI, strong colleagues?2
accessIs it eligible and practical: location, time zone, notice-period timing?2

The weights are yours. If you need income fast, raise pay and access. If you are optimising for the next three years, raise growth. The script normalises them, so any positive numbers work. The helper below also reports a close call when the top two scores are within a margin, because a 0.1 difference is noise, not an instruction.

role_scorer.py
"""A transparent weighted scorer: you set the weights, you rate each criterion 1 to 5."""


def normalise_weights(weights):
    """Return the weights scaled to sum to 1.

    Raises ValueError if any weight is negative or the total is zero.
    """
    if any(w < 0 for w in weights.values()):
        raise ValueError("weights must not be negative")
    total = sum(weights.values())
    if total <= 0:
        raise ValueError("weights must not all be zero")
    return {k: w / total for k, w in weights.items()}


def score(ratings, weights):
    """Weighted average of ratings, rounded to 2 places.

    ratings and weights are dicts keyed by criterion. Every criterion that has a
    weight must have a rating between 1 and 5 inclusive; otherwise ValueError.
    Ratings for criteria with no weight are ignored.
    """
    w = normalise_weights(weights)
    total = 0.0
    for k, share in w.items():
        if k not in ratings:
            raise ValueError("missing rating for " + k)
        r = ratings[k]
        if not 1 <= r <= 5:
            raise ValueError("rating for %s must be 1 to 5, got %r" % (k, r))
        total += r * share
    return round(total, 2)


def rank(roles, weights):
    """Return [(name, score)] sorted by score descending, ties broken by name A to Z."""
    scored = [(name, score(r, weights)) for name, r in roles.items()]
    return sorted(scored, key=lambda p: (-p[1], p[0]))


def close_call(ranked, margin=0.25):
    """True when the top two scores differ by less than margin (a tie is a close call).

    Needs at least two entries; with fewer, return False.
    """
    if len(ranked) < 2:
        return False
    return (ranked[0][1] - ranked[1][1]) < margin

Verifying a posting is open, real and legitimate

Ten minutes before you apply: is this a role someone is really hiring for, from a company that exists, contacting you the way a company would?

Is it still open?

  • Find it on the company’s own careers page, reached by typing the company’s domain yourself, not by clicking the link in a message. If it is there, that is the best signal. If the company has a careers page and the role is not on it, treat the posting as stale or unverified.
  • Read the age. A board usually shows when a posting was published or refreshed. Reposting does not reset the question of whether anyone is still reading applications.
  • Look for recent activity from the company: product updates, engineering posts, people joining. A company that has gone quiet for a long time may have frozen hiring.
  • Search the exact title plus the company to find duplicates. The same role posted by five agencies can mean one real role or none.

Is the recruiter and the company real?

  • Check the identity. A recruiter profile with a real history, connections that make sense and an email on the company’s own domain is a good sign. A free webmail address for a company recruiter, a new empty profile, or a mismatch between name and message is a reason to slow down.
  • Check the company. A website with a real product, a history, named people, and a registered business. For India-based entities, official company registries publish registration details; use the official government source and treat anything shown only on the recruiter’s own pages with caution.
  • Check independent signals. Search the company name with “scam” or “complaint”. Read employee reviews critically (see below).
  • Ask for a call or a video meeting on a named platform before sharing anything beyond your résumé. Real recruiters expect this.

One bad review proves nothing; ten that describe the same specific behaviour is a signal. Never treat a review site as verification that a company exists or that a posting is real.

Résumé and LinkedIn for this transition

Lead with evidence, state the gap in your own words, and present enterprise integration as the strength it is without borrowing credit for depth you have not built.

Principles

  • Evidence first. Put “Selected projects” with links near the top, above older employment. A reader decides in seconds; a repo link and a one-line result beat a long skills list.
  • Quantify honestly. Use numbers you can defend: tests, a corpus size, response time you measured, items processed in your demo. If you cannot reproduce a number, do not print it. Never quote employer metrics.
  • One page or two? With under five years, aim for one page. Two is acceptable if the second page earns its place with projects; padding is worse than brevity. Keep the top third strongest.
  • ATS-friendly formatting. Applicant tracking systems often parse text: use a single column, standard headings (Experience, Projects, Skills, Education), real text rather than images, a common font, and plain bullet characters. Avoid tables, text boxes and icons for essential content. Submit PDF or the format the posting asks for.
  • Tailor the top, not the whole thing. Change the two-line summary and the order of your projects per role family. Do not invent keywords to match the posting.
  • No confidential detail. Describe the type of integration (“ticketing system to storage, with retries and audit”), never client names, internal systems, volumes or code.

Portfolio presentation

A stranger gives your repository about two minutes. Make those two minutes show a working project, honestly described.

The plan does not ask you to finish learning before you apply. It asks for credible evidence: one public project that runs, with tests and an honest README. From that point, learning and applying happen in parallel, because hiring takes weeks.

Why this exists

A hiring process commonly takes several weeks from application to offer, and you cannot compress the waiting. If your first application goes out at the moment you feel ready, your first offer arrives long after that. Applying also teaches you things no study plan can: which of your claims get questions, which words recruiters respond to, and where your gaps are in practice.

A cadence you can keep

Tracking the pipeline and follow-up timing

  • After applying: wait about a week, then send one short follow-up if you have a named contact. A common range in career-centre guidance is seven to ten days; the posting’s own timeline overrides it. The log’s next_follow_up sets the date for you and moves weekend dates to Monday.
  • After an interview: send a short thank-you within a day, referencing one specific thing discussed.
  • After a silence: one polite check-in after your stated timeline; after that, mark it ghosted and move on. Repeated messages hurt more than they help.
  • Never leave a stage empty. The plan asks that no stage of the pipeline has zero items. If screens are empty, the cause is upstream.

Take-home assignments: a policy

A take-home is a trade of your unpaid time for their evaluation. Decide your policy in advance so that you are not deciding it while anxious.

SituationReasonable response
The brief states a time box of a few hours and a clear deliverableAccept. Set a timer, stay inside it, and submit what you have with notes on what you would do next.
The brief is open-ended with no time limitAsk, in writing, how long they expect it to take and what they will evaluate. A real team can answer.
It is days of work, or resembles the company’s actual backlogDecline politely and offer an alternative: a review of your existing repo, or a paid trial. You are allowed to say no.
It comes before any conversation with a humanAsk for a short call first. Round zero homework from an unknown party is a poor trade.
It involves their real data or production systemDo not accept without a written agreement. Do not use employer data or machines for it under any circumstances.

If you accept

  1. Read it twice. List deliverables, the deadline and ambiguities; email your clarifying questions early, in one message.
  2. Write the README first: what you built, how to run it, how to test it, trade-offs, what you cut and why.
  3. Build the smallest complete version before anything extra, commit as you go, and add tests for one normal case, the edges and one failure.
  4. Stop at the time box. Submit with an honest limitations section and the time you spent.
  5. Expect a follow-up conversation about it. Be ready to explain every line; do not submit code you cannot discuss.
Red pencil — the assignment that does the company’s work

If the “assignment” is a feature the company would otherwise build, and there is no time limit, no feedback and no conversation first, treat it as a red flag. Decline in writing and politely. Also: using an AI assistant on a take-home is a question to settle before you start. Ask the recruiter whether it is allowed, and say in your README what you used it for.

Scheduling and the preparation loop

Interview content lives on its own sheet: the interview sheet. This section is about the logistics around it and about learning from every loop.

  • Schedule with margin. Do not stack three loops in a day. Leave an hour before and after for preparation and a written debrief.
  • Ask what each round is. A recruiter can usually say who you meet, how long it runs and what it covers (coding, a project walkthrough, design). Prepare for exactly that.
  • Confirm the practicalities: time zone, video platform, interviewer names, whether a laptop and IDE are expected. Test your setup on a personal device and a stable connection beforehand.

Compensation: research, components, and what you can verify

This section teaches you how to find and read compensation information. It states no figures, because any number written here would be stale or wrong for your role and city. You supply the numbers, from sources you can name, on the day you need them.

Researching ranges from public sources

  1. Advertised ranges in postings (where legally or voluntarily published). They are the most direct signal, but a range is a range: the top is not a promise.
  2. Self-reported data on public sites (for example Levels.fyi and AmbitionBox) and communities. Useful for orientation; biased towards people who chose to report, and often towards larger employers. Check each site’s current terms and what is free.
  3. Government or professional-body surveys where they exist for your country and occupation.
  4. Your own network, asked respectfully and generally (“what range is typical for this scope?”), never for a named person’s salary.

Write down three things on a personal device, with the source and date of each: a floor (the lowest you would accept given your situation), a target, and the evidence behind both. The plan has you do exactly this on a Saturday in week 20.

Advertised versus estimated

Advertised

A figure or range the employer itself published in the posting or told you in writing. It is a statement from the party who will pay you. It can still be a stretch figure, may include variable pay, and may change at offer.

Estimated

A figure from a site or a model that infers pay from reports or from similar roles. Its accuracy depends on data you cannot see. It is a starting guess, never a fact about this employer.

DimensionAdvertisedEstimated
Who says itThe employerA third party
What it is based onTheir own pay bandsSelf-reports, scraped data, models
Typical errorOptimistic top of range; unclear what is includedSmall samples; wrong level; stale data
How to record it in the log“advertised, date, source”“estimated, site, date”; never in the same cell
Weight in your negotiationHigh: quote it backLow: supporting context only

Pick advertised for anything you will say aloud to the employer, and keep estimated for your own sanity checks. If you quote a number that came from a site, say that it did.

CTC components in India (awareness only)

Cost to company (CTC) is the employer’s total annual cost, not what reaches your bank account. Offers are often described in these parts. This is awareness depth: definitions, tax treatment and eligibility vary by employer, contract and law, and you must verify every item against the offer letter and ask for the full breakup in writing.

ComponentWhat it generally isWhat to ask
Fixed payThe predictable part, often split into several line items (basic, allowances and others).The full breakup, and the monthly take-home it implies after deductions.
Employer contributions and benefitsRetirement-fund and insurance items an employer pays or provides, sometimes counted inside CTC.Which benefits are inside CTC and which are on top; what each is worth to you.
Variable payPay that depends on company or individual performance.Target versus typical payout, the payout schedule, and whether a past payout can be shown.
Joining bonusA one-time amount to offset leaving your current job; often tied to staying for a period.Is it taxable, when is it paid, is there a repayment clause if you leave early.
Retention bonusPaid after a period of service.When it vests, what conditions apply.
ESOPs / equityOptions or shares that become yours over time if the company is still around.Vesting schedule, strike price, what happens if you leave, whether the company is listed. Value can be zero.

The script below is a small comparison tool. It uses abstract units (100 units = whatever your own fixed pay is), so the numbers carry no market meaning. Its value is the habit: separate what is guaranteed from what depends on performance, on staying, or on a company that may not exist in four years.

offer_breakdown.py
"""Compare two INVENTED offers by what you can count on, not by the headline number.

Units are abstract (100 units = whatever your own annual fixed pay is in the offer you hold).
These numbers are not market data and imply nothing about real pay. Every term below
(variable payout, bonus clawback, buy-out) differs by employer: the offer letter and the
employment contract decide, not this script.
"""


def breakdown(fixed, variable_target=0.0, expected_payout=0.0, joining_bonus=0.0,
              retention_bonus=0.0, esop_you_would_count=0.0):
    """Return the numbers worth comparing for one offer, all per year, in the same units.

    guaranteed    fixed pay only
    steady        fixed + variable_target * expected_payout (expected_payout is 0..1, YOUR guess)
    year_one      steady + joining bonus + retention bonus (if you stay long enough to receive them)
    with_equity   year_one + the value of equity you personally choose to count (default 0)
    """
    if not 0 <= expected_payout <= 1:
        raise ValueError("expected_payout must be between 0 and 1")
    steady = fixed + variable_target * expected_payout
    year_one = steady + joining_bonus + retention_bonus
    return {"guaranteed": round(fixed, 1), "steady": round(steady, 1),
            "year_one": round(year_one, 1), "with_equity": round(year_one + esop_you_would_count, 1)}


def buyout_example(fixed, days_to_buy_out):
    """A ROUGH illustration only: fixed pay per day times the days you would not serve.

    Real notice-period buy-outs follow your contract and the new employer's policy; some are
    fixed formulas, some are negotiated, some are not offered. Read your own contract.
    """
    return round(fixed / 365 * days_to_buy_out, 1)


offer_a = breakdown(fixed=100, variable_target=10, expected_payout=0.8)
offer_b = breakdown(fixed=90, variable_target=30, expected_payout=0.5, joining_bonus=10,
                    retention_bonus=10, esop_you_would_count=0)
for name, o in (("Offer A", offer_a), ("Offer B", offer_b)):
    print(name, o)

print("buy-out illustration for 30 days:", buyout_example(100, 30), "units")
print("Offer B headline looks bigger in year one, but only", offer_b["guaranteed"],
      "is guaranteed, and the bonuses assume you stay and the business performs.")

Negotiation principles

Negotiation is a calm conversation about a problem you are solving together: a role that is worth what it costs to fill. It is a skill, so rehearse it aloud before you need it. The plan has you do that on day 146.

  1. Anchor on researched ranges. Name your source: “the advertised range for this role was X to Y; given my scope and evidence I am looking for Z.” Do not invent a number.
  2. Ask, do not demand. “Is there flexibility on the fixed component?” works better than an ultimatum, and costs you nothing.
  3. Think in total compensation: fixed, variable, joining and retention terms, equity, start date, remote terms, learning budget, title. Sometimes the part they can move is not the part you first asked about.
  4. Never accept or decline on the first call. Say thank you, say you are excited, and ask for the offer in writing and a day or two to review. This is normal and respected.
  5. Get it in writing. A verbal figure is not an offer. Do not resign, give notice or turn down other processes until you hold a written offer you have read.
  6. Do not lie. Do not invent a competing offer. If you do have a deadline from another process, say so truthfully.
  7. Know your floor and your walk-away before the conversation, and what you would trade for what.
phrases to rehearse aloud
Receiving the offer:
  "Thank you, I am glad to have this. Could you send the details in writing? I would like to
   review the full breakup and come back to you by [day]."

Asking about flexibility:
  "Based on the advertised range and the scope we discussed, I was hoping for [figure].
   Is there flexibility on the fixed component?"

If the answer is no:
  "Understood. Is there flexibility on [joining bonus / start date / review timing / title]?"

Closing:
  "Thank you for working through this. If we can confirm [agreed terms] in the written offer, I am happy to accept."
Interviewers ask

“What are your salary expectations?” Early in the process, ask about the budgeted range for the role first. If pressed, give a range anchored on the advertised or researched figures and say it depends on the total package and scope. Do not lie about your current pay or reveal confidential details of it where you can decline. In India, asking for current CTC is common; you may politely say you prefer to talk about the role’s range. Follow-up: “what is your current CTC?” Decide beforehand how you will answer truthfully and what you will not volunteer.

Red pencil — negotiating before you understand the offer

Do not counter until you have the whole offer in writing and understand each component. Countering a number you have misread, or accepting verbally and then raising extra requests, damages trust. Never signal that you will accept before you have decided.

Evaluating offers: a decision matrix

An offer can feel exciting and be wrong, or feel disappointing and be right. A written matrix, filled from written facts, makes the comparison something you can argue with instead of a mood.

Hard filters first, then weights

Before any scoring, confirm the hard filters. If any fails, the offer is out or paused until it is fixed: the legal employer is who you think it is, the offer is in writing and complete, the location and remote terms are explicit, and the notice and start date are workable. Then score each offer 1 to 5 on the criteria below, with weights you set before you look at the offers.

The same role_scorer.py above computes this matrix: its last demo scores two invented offers with these exact criteria and weights and gets 3.67 and 3.5, which the script flags as a close call (a gap under 0.25). Use it, then do the part the arithmetic cannot do: talk to the people you would work with once more, and ask for anything unclear in writing.

Interviewers ask

“Why do you want to leave your current role?” Answer with what you are moving towards (a role where you build and ship AI systems on the integration foundation you have) rather than complaints about your employer. Be truthful, brief and calm. Never disparage a current employer or share anything confidential. Follow-up: “what would make you stay?” Name one concrete, honest thing.

Notice periods and counter-offers

Awareness only. What applies to you is in your own employment contract. Read it before you start interviewing, not after you have an offer.

  • Read your contract for the notice period, whether it is negotiable, any buy-out clause, garden-leave or non-compete terms, and what happens to unpaid variable pay or benefits. Do not assume it matches what colleagues say or what is typical.
  • Tell recruiters your notice period early and exactly. A long notice period can affect which roles are practical. Ask whether the new employer would wait, or would consider a buy-out.
  • A buy-out is a negotiation, not a right, and may be paid by you, by the new employer, or not offered. Get any agreement in writing from the party who pays.
  • Resign professionally. Give written notice only after a signed written offer, hand over cleanly, return equipment, and do not take or forward any employer material.

Counter-offers

Your current employer may respond to your resignation with a counter-offer. Decide your position before you resign. Questions to ask yourself, in writing: why was I leaving in the first place? Does the counter-offer fix that reason, or only the number? Would the new role still be the better move on the matrix? Would a promise of change be in writing? There is no universal rule, and anyone who gives you one does not know your situation. Do not use a real offer as a bluff to extract a counter-offer, and do not assume a counter-offer is available.

Red pencil — resigning on a verbal offer

Resigning before you hold a signed written offer, with a verified legal entity and the terms you agreed, is the one irreversible mistake in this whole process. Offers have been delayed, changed or withdrawn. Keep your current job until the paper is in your hands and, where relevant, background checks are complete.

International remote roles: what to check

“Remote” describes where you sit, not who may employ you. A company can be happy for you to work from home and still be unable, or unwilling, to hire in India. Check five things in writing before you invest in a loop.

Contractor or employee

Employee (direct or through an employer of record)

A contract of employment. Pay usually runs through payroll with deductions handled by the employer or the agent. Benefits and leave follow the contract and local rules. An employer of record (EOR) is a third party that legally employs you on behalf of a foreign company; check that it is a registered, verifiable firm.

Contractor (independent)

A services agreement. You invoice, you usually handle your own taxes and registrations, and benefits and leave are yours to arrange. Rules on what counts as a genuine contractor differ by country, and some arrangements described as contracting may be treated differently by law.

DimensionEmployee (direct or EOR)Contractor

Choose by reading the actual agreement. Neither is better in the abstract; what matters is the paper and your own tax position.

Payments, fees and time zones

  • Payment route: ask how and when you are paid and in which currency. Bank transfers, payment platforms and payroll providers each have fees and exchange-rate margins. Get the total you will actually receive in rupees, not just the headline figure.
  • Time zones: convert required overlap hours to Indian time and picture them on a normal Tuesday. A role that needs you on calls from late evening to early morning is a different job from one that overlaps your afternoon.
  • Equipment and data: who supplies the laptop, and what security rules apply to your home network and devices.
Tax: take professional advice

How income from a foreign employer or client is taxed in India, which registrations you need as a contractor, and what a foreign company must withhold are legal questions that depend on your situation and on current rules. Do not rely on this sheet, a recruiter or a forum for them. Speak to a qualified tax professional before you sign a cross-border agreement.

Red pencil — “remote” in the headline, “US residents only” in the footer

Read to the bottom of the posting. Work-authorisation and location restrictions are often at the end or in a linked page, and a posting on a global board may be aimed at a single country. If the text says nothing about India, ask. Spending three weeks in a loop for a role that cannot hire you is the most expensive way to learn that.

Protecting confidentiality

Your employer’s information is not yours to bring into a job search. Breaking this rule can cost you an offer, your current job, or worse, and it is entirely avoidable.

Red pencil — the demo that is secretly employer work

A portfolio project “inspired by work” that reproduces an employer’s data model, naming or workflow is employer information, even if you rewrote every line. Start from a public problem and synthetic data. If in doubt, read your contract’s confidentiality and intellectual-property clauses, and ask a lawyer or HR in general terms rather than guessing.

Pacing, rejection and the weekly review

Rejection is the normal output of the funnel, not a measurement of your worth. A search you can sustain for months beats a sprint that ends in exhaustion.

  • Expect most applications to end in silence or a no. The log makes that visible as a rate, not a personal event.
  • Work set hours. A fixed number of search hours each week, with a clear start and stop, protects your current job, your sleep and your learning.
  • Keep one full pause per week. The plan makes Saturday the review and the pause day for this reason.
  • Change one thing at a time. If five weeks of applications produced silence, change the targeting or the résumé and watch the next batch. Changing everything teaches you nothing.
  • Tell someone. A friend or mentor who asks “what did the log say this week?” keeps you honest. If stress becomes persistent low mood, poor sleep or hopelessness, speak to a doctor or a counsellor; that is a health matter, not a productivity one.

Interview-style Q&A: walk me through your transition

This is the question that opens most first calls. Prepare it, say it aloud, and make every sentence one you can defend.

Interviewers ask

“Walk me through your transition from enterprise integration into applied AI.” A defensible shape, in about 90 seconds: (1) What I did: four years connecting enterprise systems, APIs, auth, retries, data mapping, audit. (2) What I saw: the hard part of AI in organisations is the integration, permissions and reliability around the model. (3) What I did about it: a structured programme of Python, then two public projects you can open (one sentence each, with the honest limit of each). (4) What I am looking for: a role where I build and ship AI systems and keep learning, and what I bring that a model-only candidate may not. (5) Close with a question about the team. Follow-ups to prepare: “how much Python do you have, honestly?” (months of focused work, here are the repos); “what did an AI assistant write versus you?” (answer from your ownership lines); “why leave now?” (towards, not away from).

Red pencil — the rehearsed story that grows

Every retelling tempts you to round up: a month becomes a quarter, “contributed” becomes “led”. Write the 90-second version once, keep it on a personal device, and tell that version every time. Consistency across five interviewers is the best evidence you are not embellishing.

Drill — job search method
You meet seven of ten listed requirements. Do you apply?

It depends on which three you miss, not on the count. Separate required from nice-to-have, and check whether the missing ones are core to the responsibilities. If the gaps are secondary, learnable and you can state them plainly, apply. If a core requirement is missing and you cannot evidence it, log the gap and skip or build the evidence first. The percentage from a keyword matcher does not decide this.

Why does the keyword matcher separate “unevidenced” from “gaps”?

A term you list without a link is a claim, not evidence, so it must not raise your coverage. Showing it separately reminds you either to produce the link or to remove the claim from your résumé. Follow-up: “what does the helper miss?” Skills not in the vocabulary, required versus preferred, and depth.

How do you check that a job posting is real and still open?

Find it on the company’s own careers page by typing the domain myself, check the recruiter’s identity and email domain, look at the posting date and company activity, search for duplicates and complaints, and ask for a video call before sharing anything sensitive. Any request for a fee, early ID or bank details, or a move to an off-platform chat stops the process.

A role says “remote”. What do you check before applying from India?

The location line, the employment type (employee, contractor, EOR), payroll and tax handling, the required time-zone overlap in IST, and the legal entity that would sign the contract. If any is unclear I ask in writing before the loop, and I take professional tax advice before signing a cross-border agreement.

Advertised versus estimated compensation: which do you quote, and why?

I quote advertised figures, since the employer itself stated them. Estimated figures from sites rely on self-reports and models, so I use them only for private orientation and say so if I mention them. In the log I record the type, source and date in separate fields so they are never mixed.

An offer has a large joining bonus and a high variable component. Why is the headline a poor comparison?

Only the fixed pay is guaranteed. Variable pay depends on performance and company results, and a joining bonus often has a repayment clause if you leave early. Compare guaranteed, expected steady-state, and year-one-if-I-stay separately, count equity at zero unless I have a reason, and verify every component against the offer letter.

The recruiter phones with a verbal offer and wants an answer today. What do you do?

Thank them, say I am keen, and ask for the full offer in writing and a day or two to review. I do not accept, decline or resign on the call. A real employer expects this; pressure to answer immediately is itself a warning sign.

When do you decline a take-home?

When it is days of work, has no time limit, resembles the company’s real backlog, comes before any human conversation, or uses their real data without an agreement. I ask first about expected time and evaluation criteria and offer an alternative such as a review of my existing repository.

How do you describe enterprise integration experience without overclaiming?

I state exactly what I did (APIs, auth, retries, mapping, audit), what I did not (production AI systems in Python), and what I built since, with links. I add an ownership line to each project saying what I wrote, what an AI tool drafted and what I can explain, and I never include client names, data or code.

Your employer counters your resignation. How do you decide?

I decide before resigning. I ask why I was leaving, whether the counter addresses that reason or only the number, whether promises are in writing, and whether the new role still wins on my matrix. I check my contract and do not assume a counter will be offered or that it is better. I never bluff with a fake offer.

Five weeks of applications produced almost no replies. What do you change first?

Use the log to find where the funnel leaks. Silence before screens points at targeting or the résumé; so I check role fit, eligibility and whether the top third of the résumé shows linked evidence. I change one thing, apply a fresh batch and compare response rates, rather than changing everything at once.
