The five rounds

A senior loop is six conversations, five of which are scored, spread over three to five weeks. Each one has a stated task and an actual assessment, and they are never the same thing. Read this drawing until you can name the assessment under every stated task.

1
The recruiter screen is a gate, not a round. Nobody is testing your engineering. They are testing whether you are cheap to represent: can you say what you do in ninety seconds, are you the level they are hiring, will the numbers work, and will you actually turn up. Most of the damage done here is done to your compensation, not your candidacy — see the logistics section below before you take this call.
2
The technical screen buys one thing: permission to spend senior time on you. A live 60-minute screen is usually one medium problem plus a few API questions. A take-home is the same signal in a different shape, and the shape is a trap: candidates spend four days on something budgeted for four hours and still ship it without a README. Cap it, say what you capped, and write down what you would do next.
3
The coding round is a communication test disguised as an algorithms test. Two candidates produce identical code; the one who clarified the input, said the complexity out loud before typing, and found their own off-by-one gets the hire. At senior level, an elegant solution produced in silence scores below a straightforward solution produced out loud.
4
System design is a scoping test. The question is deliberately too big for 45 minutes, so the first thing being measured is whether you notice and cut it down. Interviewers are listening for numbers, for a data model, and for the sentence “here is what I am trading away”. Three fully worked designs are below; the first is BadgeDesk, which you built, and is therefore the one you can answer better than any other candidate in the pipeline.
5
The deep dive is the only deterministic round. Its content is your CV, which you wrote. Every noun on it — Kafka, pgvector, Terraform, EKS — is a question you can predict word for word. Remove anything you cannot defend two questions past the summary, then drill the rest. Thirty of those questions with real answers are below.
6
Behavioural is not the soft round. At SE2 it is a formality; at SE3 it is where most rejections and almost all down-levellings happen, because the whole level difference is scope, ownership and influence, and those only show up in stories. Six pre-written stories, drilled aloud, are worth more than twenty extra LeetCode problems.
7
Assume you will be rejected somewhere, and instrument it. Ask the recruiter — never the interviewer — for written feedback within 24 hours while you are still fresh in their notes. Log the round, the question and the failure in one file. Three processes running in parallel turns each rejection into data instead of a mood, and it is also the only honest source of leverage you will have on compensation.
Why this sheet exists

Knowing and performing are different skills with different training. You can hold every idea on Sheets 02 to 12 and still fail a loop, because an interview adds three things a tutorial never does: a clock, an audience, and a question you were not expecting. The only training for those is repetition under the same conditions — timed, out loud, recorded. Everything on this sheet is designed to be rehearsed aloud, not read.

Red pencil — the preparation that feels productive and is not

Reading solutions instead of writing them; watching system design videos instead of drawing; rehearsing stories in your head instead of out loud. All three feel like work and produce almost no transfer, because recognition is not recall and thinking is not speaking. The test for whether preparation counts: did something leave your body — typed code, a drawn diagram, a spoken sentence someone else could hear. If not, it was revision theatre.

Coding rounds — the honest minimum

Nine patterns cover almost every backend coding round. Sixty problems chosen across those nine, solved properly and revisited, beats three hundred skimmed — and for a senior role the code you write matters less than the way you write it.

Why sixty and not three hundred

The return on problem number two hundred is not the pattern — you learned that at problem forty — it is speed on a distribution you will not see anyway. Sixty problems spread across nine patterns gives you roughly six exposures per pattern, which is where recall becomes reliable. The remaining hours buy far more if you spend them on system design and stories, because those rounds have more variance and almost nobody prepares them properly. If you have already done three hundred and still freeze, the problem was never the count.

A worked example, done properly

The problem below is deliberately BadgeDesk-shaped, because interval problems are what an access-control domain generates and because you can talk about the production version afterwards. Read the whole walk-through as a transcript: the words matter as much as the code.

Interviewers ask

“An employee has been granted several access windows at one site, each a start date and an end date, both inclusive. They overlap and duplicate. Return the minimal set of windows that grants exactly the same access.”

Step 1 — clarify. Four questions, ninety seconds, and you write the answers down where the interviewer can see them. Never more than five; asking eight questions is its own failure mode.

the first ninety seconds, out loud
You  : Are these all for one employee at one site, or mixed?
Them : One employee, one site. Assume they are pre-filtered.

You  : Are they sorted? Can I mutate the input?
Them : Not sorted. Yes, you can mutate it.

You  : Both ends inclusive? So 1-3 Sep means the badge works on the 3rd?
Them : Correct.

You  : Do two windows that touch count as one? 1-3 Sep and 4-6 Sep -
       there is no day in between, so access is continuous.
Them : Good catch. Yes, treat touching as overlapping.

You  : Roughly how many windows? Does this need to stream?
Them : A few thousand per employee. It fits in memory.

You  : Then I will sort by start date and sweep once, merging as I go.

Step 2 — state the approach and the complexity before typing. Say this sentence, in these words, and then wait for the nod:

the approach, spoken
"Sort the windows by start date. Then walk them once, keeping the last
 window I have committed to the output. For each next window, if it
 starts on or before the day after that committed window ends, they are
 continuous, so I extend the committed window's end date to whichever
 end is later. Otherwise there is a real gap, so I commit a new window.

 That is O(n log n) time - the sort dominates, the sweep is O(n) - and
 O(n) space for the output, with O(1) extra beyond it if I sort in place.

 The correctness argument is that after sorting by start, any window that
 overlaps the committed one must start inside or immediately after it, so
 a single backward-looking comparison is enough."

Step 3 — write it. Production-shaped code, not competition code: a real type, a docstring with the complexity in it, and a comment only where the reasoning is not visible.

badgedesk/core/windows.py
from dataclasses import dataclass
from datetime import date, timedelta

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Window:
    starts_on: date
    ends_on: date

def merge_windows(windows: list[Window]) -> list[Window]:
    """Collapse overlapping or touching access windows into the minimal set.

    Time:  O(n log n) - the sort dominates; the sweep is O(n).
    Space: O(n) for the output, O(1) extra beyond it.
    """
    if not windows:                       # edge case 1: nothing granted
        return []

    ordered = sorted(windows, key=lambda w: (w.starts_on, w.ends_on))
    merged = [ordered[0]]

    for window in ordered[1:]:
        last = merged[-1]

        # Touching counts as overlapping: 1-3 Sep and 4-6 Sep are continuous
        # access, so the boundary is `last.ends_on + 1 day`, not `last.ends_on`.
        if window.starts_on <= last.ends_on + timedelta(days=1):
            if window.ends_on > last.ends_on:
                merged[-1] = Window(last.starts_on, window.ends_on)
            # else: this window is entirely inside the last one - drop it
        else:
            merged.append(window)         # a real gap in access

    return merged

Step 5 — optimise, and know when not to. The honest answer is that O(n log n) is optimal in general, and saying so confidently is worth more than a wrong speed-up. Then offer the two directions the interviewer is actually fishing for:

If the input is boundedBucket by day, O(n + D)

Access windows live inside a two-year calendar, so D is at most 730 days. Mark a boolean array and scan it once. Faster than sorting, and it beats the comparison-sort lower bound only because the domain is small — say that explicitly, it is the point.

If the input streamsKeep it sorted on arrival

If windows arrive one at a time, a sorted structure or a balanced interval tree gives O(log n) insert with merge, so you never re-sort. This is the “what if it does not fit in memory” answer too: sort externally, then sweep, because the sweep itself is streaming.

If it is really productionPush it into Postgres

The best version of this code is the code you do not write. Postgres daterange plus a GiST exclusion constraint makes an overlapping window impossible to insert, so the merge never needs to run defensively.

Red pencil — silent coding

The single most common way a competent engineer fails a coding round is going quiet. It feels efficient — you are concentrating — but from the other side of the call the interviewer sees a blank screen, no signal, and a candidate they cannot help. Three habits fix it permanently. One: narrate the next line before you write it (“now I need the committed window, so I take merged[-1]”). Two: when you get stuck, say what you are stuck on out loud — “I am deciding whether to handle the contained case here or after the loop” — which converts silence into signal and usually earns you a free hint. Three: never say “done”; say “let me run the example through it”. If you would be embarrassed to narrate it, that is the bug.

The estimation cheat sheet

Two sets of numbers make the difference between a design that sounds plausible and one that is defensible: latencies you know cold, and the arithmetic that turns users into requests per second and bytes per year. Round everything. Nobody is checking your multiplication; they are checking whether the conclusion follows.

Red pencil — designing for traffic nobody mentioned

The most common design failure at SE3 is not under-engineering, it is reflex over-engineering: reaching for sharding, Cassandra, a service mesh and a Kafka cluster for a system the interviewer told you has 200,000 users. It reads as pattern-matching rather than judgement, and it is easy to expose — one question about how you would re-shard, or what a mesh costs you operationally, and the answer thins out. Do the arithmetic first, then let the number choose the architecture, and say the words “this does not need…” at least once. Deliberately declining complexity is a stronger signal than deploying it.

Worked design 1 — BadgeDesk

The design you can answer better than anyone else in the pipeline, because you built it, ran it, and broke it. Every other candidate is reciting a blog post about designing a URL shortener; you are describing a system with your fingerprints on it.

Requirements, in ninety seconds

FunctionalWhat it must do

An employee requests access to a site for a date range. Their manager approves or rejects. An approved request provisions a badge in the vendor access-control system. Security can see every badge event. A nightly job proves BadgeDesk and the vendor still agree.

Non-functionalWhat it must hold

Request submission answers in under 200 ms even when the vendor is down. Approvals are auditable forever. No badge is ever provisioned twice. Reconciliation completes inside the 02:00–04:00 window. Availability target 99.9%, which is 43 minutes a month.

Explicitly out of scopeWhat you refuse to design

Visitor management, turnstile firmware, HR joiner-mover-leaver sync, and the mobile app. Say this out loud in the first two minutes — declaring scope you are not building is half the scoping mark.

The architecture, drawn in the order you narrate it

The numbered callouts below are the order you draw the boxes on the whiteboard, not a legend. Practise until you can produce this drawing from an empty board in four minutes while talking.

1–3
The synchronous spine, and why it is short. Browser, gateway, core. The gateway does authentication and rate limiting so the core never sees an unauthenticated request; the core evaluates site policy and writes one row. Three hops, one human waiting, target 200 ms. Everything slow has been deliberately pushed off this path.
4
Postgres is the source of truth and the reason is relational. A request belongs to an employee, an approval belongs to a request, a badge belongs to both. Those are foreign keys and a transaction, not documents. Audit events go to Mongo instead because their shape differs per vendor — naming that split unprompted is worth a mark.
5
Redis holds two things you can afford to lose. The Idempotency-Key from the client, so a double-clicked submit creates one request and not two; and the site_id → policy cache with a 60-second TTL, because policy changes weekly and is read on every submission. If Redis is empty the system is slower, never wrong. Say that sentence.
6
The seam — this is the whole design. Provisioning means calling a vendor that is slow and sometimes down. So the API does not call it. It writes the row, publishes to badge.requests and returns 202 Accepted with a status URL. Partitioning by employee_id keeps one person's events ordered without needing global ordering, which is the trade you should name out loud.
7
The reconciler is the honesty mechanism. Every night it reads what BadgeDesk believes and what the vendor actually has, and writes the differences to S3. Without it, an at-least-once pipeline with a flaky downstream drifts silently for months. Interviewers love this box because most candidates never draw it.
8
Failure is a destination, not an absence. Three retries with exponential backoff and jitter, then the message lands in a dead-letter topic with an alarm on depth greater than zero. “What happens when the vendor is down for six hours?” is the follow-up, and the answer is: nothing is lost, the queue grows, badges provision late, and one alarm fires.

Data model and the one index you can justify

badgedesk/db/schema.sql — the five minutes of the round spent here
CREATE TABLE access_requests (
  id           uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  employee_id  text        NOT NULL REFERENCES employees(id),
  site_id      text        NOT NULL REFERENCES sites(id),
  starts_on    date        NOT NULL,
  ends_on      date        NOT NULL,
  status       text        NOT NULL DEFAULT 'pending',
  reason       text        NOT NULL,
  created_at   timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  CHECK (ends_on >= starts_on),
  CHECK (status IN ('pending', 'approved', 'rejected'))
);

CREATE TABLE approvals (
  request_id   uuid PRIMARY KEY REFERENCES access_requests(id),
  approver_id  text        NOT NULL REFERENCES employees(id),
  decision     text        NOT NULL,
  decided_at   timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);   -- one approval per request: the PK enforces "cannot approve twice"

CREATE TABLE badges (
  id           uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  request_id   uuid UNIQUE NOT NULL REFERENCES access_requests(id),
  vendor_ref   text UNIQUE,            -- NULL until the vendor confirms
  provisioned_at timestamptz
);   -- UNIQUE(request_id) is how "never provision twice" survives a retry

-- The manager dashboard polls this every 30 s. A partial index over the ~2%
-- of rows that are pending is 50x smaller than the full index and stays hot.
CREATE INDEX idx_requests_pending
    ON access_requests (site_id, created_at DESC)
    WHERE status = 'pending';

-- 175 GB/year of badge taps, queried almost always by month: partition it.
CREATE TABLE access_events (
  employee_id text NOT NULL, site_id text NOT NULL,
  occurred_at timestamptz NOT NULL, outcome text NOT NULL
) PARTITION BY RANGE (occurred_at);

The follow-ups the interviewer will ask about BadgeDesk
The vendor API is down for six hours. Walk me through exactly what happens.

Submissions and approvals are unaffected — they never touch the vendor. The provisioning worker's calls time out at five seconds, it retries three times with exponential backoff and jitter, then publishes to badge.requests.dlq. Consumer lag on badge.requests climbs and an alarm fires at five minutes of lag; DLQ depth greater than zero pages. Badges provision late, nothing is lost, and the status endpoint shows provisioning. When the vendor recovers I replay the DLQ, which is safe because the UNIQUE(request_id) on badges makes provisioning idempotent. The follow-up is “how do you stop retries hammering a recovering vendor” — a circuit breaker and jitter, so 4,000 queued messages do not arrive in the same second.

Two managers approve the same request at the same instant. What happens?

Nothing bad, because approvals.request_id is the primary key. The second transaction gets a unique-violation and the API maps it to 409 Conflict with a body saying who approved it. The alternative — checking “is it still pending?” in application code then writing — is a read-modify-write race under Read Committed and will let both through occasionally. The database constraint is the correctness boundary; the application check is only there for a nicer error message.

How do you make provisioning exactly-once when Kafka is at-least-once?

You do not; you make the effect idempotent. The worker sends the request id to the vendor as its idempotency key and writes badges(request_id) under a unique constraint, so a redelivered message either finds the badge already there or loses the insert race and exits. Effectively once, delivered at least once. If the vendor did not support idempotency keys I would do a read-before-write against the vendor plus the unique constraint, and accept a small window where a duplicate is created and reconciled away that night.

This grows to 50 tenants and 10 million employees. What breaks first?

Not the write path — that is still under 100 writes a second. The first thing to break is access_events at roughly 9 TB a year, which forces partition pruning to be correct and pushes old partitions to cold storage. Second is the manager dashboard's polling, which I would move to server-sent events or a longer TTL cache. Third, and the real problem, is tenant isolation: one tenant's replay of a million events starving everyone else's provisioning. That is a per-tenant partition or a separate consumer group with quotas, not a bigger database.

Why not put the whole thing in one service?

For 200,000 users, a modular monolith would genuinely be a defensible choice, and I would say so. The split earns its keep in exactly one place: the provisioning worker has a completely different failure profile and scaling need from the API — it blocks on a slow third party for seconds at a time. Isolating that is worth one process boundary. The gateway and core could reasonably be one deployable. Being willing to argue against your own architecture is usually the strongest thirty seconds of the round.

Worked design 2 — a distributed rate limiter

The most commonly asked “small” design question, and the one that quietly tests whether you understand atomicity. In BadgeDesk it lives in the gateway, in front of POST /requests, so one script cannot submit ten thousand access requests in a minute.

Requirements and the numbers

FunctionalLimit by what, exactly

Per employee: 30 submissions a minute with a burst of 20. Per tenant: 2,000 a minute. Per IP on the unauthenticated login route: 5 a minute. Different rules per route, changeable without a deploy, and a 429 response that tells the client when to come back.

Non-functionalThe hard constraint

It sits on every request, so it must cost under 1 ms at p99 and must not be a single point of failure. Approximate is acceptable; slow is not. Decide up front whether you fail open or fail closed when the counter store is unreachable — that is a business answer, not a technical one.

ScaleWhy it cannot be local

Six gateway pods behind a load balancer, autoscaling to twenty. Any counter held in process is multiplied by the replica count and reset by every deploy. This single observation is the reason the question exists.

Four algorithms, drawn

Sliding window counter

Two integers per key and one round trip. Smooths the boundary burst down to about a 1% error, which nobody will ever notice or complain about. It has no concept of a burst allowance: 30 a minute means you cannot do 20 in the first second even if you have been idle for an hour.

Shape of the traffic it produces: flat.

Token bucket

Two fields per key, refilled lazily on read, so it costs the same. Explicitly separates sustained rate from burst size, which is what an API client actually wants: idle for an hour, then fire twenty in a second, then settle down to the steady rate.

Shape of the traffic it produces: bursty but bounded.

Where the counters live, and the race that eats them

badgedesk/gateway/rate_limit.py — token bucket, atomic, one round trip
import time
import redis
from fastapi import Request, HTTPException

# Redis runs this script single-threaded, so the whole refill-and-decide is atomic.
TOKEN_BUCKET = """
local capacity    = tonumber(ARGV[1])
local refill_rate = tonumber(ARGV[2])   -- tokens per second
local now_ms      = tonumber(ARGV[3])
local cost        = tonumber(ARGV[4])

local state   = redis.call('HMGET', KEYS[1], 'tokens', 'ts')
local tokens  = tonumber(state[1])
local last_ms = tonumber(state[2])

if tokens == nil then                   -- first request for this key
  tokens  = capacity
  last_ms = now_ms
end

-- Refill lazily for the time that has passed, capped at the bucket size.
local elapsed = math.max(0, now_ms - last_ms) / 1000
tokens = math.min(capacity, tokens + elapsed * refill_rate)

local allowed = 0
if tokens >= cost then
  tokens  = tokens - cost
  allowed = 1
end

redis.call('HSET', KEYS[1], 'tokens', tokens, 'ts', now_ms)
-- Expire after a full refill would have happened anyway: idle keys cost nothing.
redis.call('PEXPIRE', KEYS[1], math.ceil(capacity / refill_rate * 1000) + 1000)

return { allowed, math.floor(tokens) }
"""

_redis  = redis.Redis(host="badgedesk-redis", socket_timeout=0.05)
_bucket = _redis.register_script(TOKEN_BUCKET)

def check(key: str, capacity: int, per_second: float, cost: int = 1):
    """Returns (allowed, tokens_left). Fails OPEN if Redis is unreachable."""
    try:
        allowed, left = _bucket(keys=[f"rl:{key}"],
                                args=[capacity, per_second, int(time.time() * 1000), cost])
        return bool(allowed), int(left)
    except redis.RedisError:
        # Business decision, made deliberately: a rate limiter must never be
        # the reason the product is down. Log it, emit a metric, let it through.
        return True, -1

async def limit_submissions(request: Request):
    employee_id = request.state.employee_id
    allowed, left = check(f"req:{employee_id}", capacity=20, per_second=0.5)  # 30/min
    if not allowed:
        raise HTTPException(
            status_code=429,
            detail="Too many access requests. Try again shortly.",
            headers={"Retry-After": "2", "X-RateLimit-Remaining": "0"},
        )
Red pencil — fail open or fail closed, and never shrugging

“What happens when Redis is down?” is the follow-up in every single rate-limiter interview, and “then rate limiting stops working” is not an answer. Say which way you fail and why the business chose it: a limiter protecting an expensive downstream fails closed, because letting everything through takes the real system down with it; a limiter protecting against casual abuse fails open, because a Redis blip should never take the product offline. Then add the mitigation — a 50 ms timeout so a slow Redis cannot become your latency, and a small in-process fallback counter so failing open is not unlimited.

Rate limiter follow-ups
Where should the limiter sit — edge, gateway or service?

All three, for different reasons. The CDN or load balancer handles volumetric abuse cheaply and never reaches your compute. The gateway does per-identity limits, because that is the first place identity is known. The service does expensive-operation limits — a report export costing ten tokens instead of one — because only the service knows the cost. If you can only build one, build the gateway one.

How do you tell the client what happened?

429 Too Many Requests, with Retry-After in seconds, plus X-RateLimit-Limit, -Remaining and -Reset. With a token bucket you can compute Retry-After exactly: the tokens still needed divided by the refill rate. The follow-up is “why not 503?” — because 503 means the server is unwell, and 429 means the client is; that distinction drives whether their client retries the same request or backs off entirely.

One tenant is being throttled but the whole cluster feels slow. What did you get wrong?

Almost certainly hot-keying: every request for a large tenant hashes to a single Redis key on a single shard, so that one slot saturates. The fixes are to shard the counter into N sub-keys and allow limit/N on each, or to move to a local-token-lease model where each pod leases a block of tokens and only talks to Redis when the block runs out — less exact, dramatically fewer round trips.

Can you do this without a central store at all?

Yes, approximately. Each pod enforces limit divided by replica count, or pods gossip counts every few hundred milliseconds. Both trade accuracy for latency and are what you reach for when the limiter must be sub-100 microseconds or must survive a regional Redis outage. Name the trade explicitly: you are choosing to be wrong by a bounded amount in exchange for never making a network call.

Worked design 3 — a notification service

The design that looks easy and is not. Fan-out, four channels, templating, user preferences, deduplication, retries and a delivery guarantee you have to state out loud. In BadgeDesk this is the notifier, grown up.

Requirements, estimation and the one hard number

FunctionalWhat it must do

Any service publishes a domain event. The notification service decides who should hear about it, on which channels — email, Teams, SMS, outbound webhook — renders a versioned template in the right locale, respects user preferences and quiet hours, delivers it once, and records what happened.

Non-functionalWhat it must hold

A badge-approved email arrives within 30 seconds at p95. A site-closure broadcast to 50,000 people completes within 5 minutes. No user ever receives the same notification twice. Security alerts ignore quiet hours and preferences — that exemption must be in the model, not in a special case.

The one hard numberFan-out, not volume

Steady state is trivial: 1,200,000 badge events a day produce maybe 20,000 notifications, under 1 per second. The design exists for the spike: one operator clicks “close BLR-01” and 50,000 messages must exist in the next few seconds. Design for the fan-out, not the average.

Fan-out: on write or on read

Fan-out on write — push

When the event arrives, expand it to every recipient immediately and write one delivery per person. Reads are then trivial: the message already exists. Cost is a write amplification of 50,000 for a site-wide closure, and a badly-chosen audience is expensive to undo.

Right for notifications, because delivery is the product — an email that exists only when someone opens an app is not a notification.

Fan-out on read — pull

Store the event once and compute each person's list when they open the in-app inbox. One write, cheap spikes, always current with preferences. But nothing is pushed anywhere, and computing the list on every read is expensive for users in many groups.

Right for the in-app bell icon, and for celebrity-style audiences where the same event is relevant to enormous numbers of people.

badgedesk/notifier/worker.py — dedupe, send, retry, give up loudly
import hashlib, json, random
from confluent_kafka import Consumer, Producer

DEDUPE_TTL   = 86_400          # 24 h - longer than any retry chain can live
MAX_ATTEMPTS = 5

def dedupe_key(event_id: str, employee_id: str, channel: str) -> str:
    raw = f"{event_id}:{employee_id}:{channel}".encode()
    return "dedupe:" + hashlib.sha256(raw).hexdigest()[:32]

def backoff_seconds(attempt: int) -> float:
    """Exponential with full jitter, so 4,000 retries do not arrive together."""
    return random.uniform(0, min(300, 2 ** attempt))

def handle(message, redis_client, producer: Producer) -> None:
    event = json.loads(message.value())
    key   = dedupe_key(event["event_id"], event["employee_id"], event["channel"])

    # SET NX is the whole deduplication story: at-least-once delivery means this
    # message WILL sometimes arrive twice. The first writer wins; the rest exit.
    if not redis_client.set(key, "1", nx=True, ex=DEDUPE_TTL):
        return

    try:
        provider_id = send_via_channel(event)            # 5 s timeout, always
        record_delivery(event, status="sent", provider_id=provider_id)
    except TransientProviderError:
        redis_client.delete(key)                         # let the retry try again
        event["attempt"] = event.get("attempt", 0) + 1
        if event["attempt"] < MAX_ATTEMPTS:
            event["not_before"] = now() + backoff_seconds(event["attempt"])
            producer.produce("notify.retry", json.dumps(event))
        else:
            record_delivery(event, status="failed")
            producer.produce("notify.dlq", json.dumps(event))   # never dropped
    except PermanentProviderError:                       # bad address, opted out
        record_delivery(event, status="bounced")         # no retry, no DLQ
In production — what “delivery guarantee” actually means

Say this in the round and then stop talking: the pipeline is at-least-once, the effect is effectively-once, and end-to-end exactly-once does not exist because the last hop is a third party who may accept your message and then fail before telling you. Everything else follows: consumers must be idempotent, deduplication is a normal operating condition rather than an error path, and the honest guarantee to a user is “you will get this at least once, and we work hard so it is exactly once”. Candidates who claim exactly-once get one follow-up question and lose the round on it.

Notification service follow-ups
The email provider throttles you at 14 messages a second. How does 50,000 get out?

It does not go out in a second, and pretending otherwise is the wrong answer: 50,000 at 14 per second is roughly an hour. So I would put the rate limit in the worker — a token bucket shared across the worker pods, exactly the design above — and let the Kafka partition act as the buffer. Then I would prioritise: security alerts on a separate topic with its own consumer group so a marketing-shaped backlog cannot delay them, and a per-channel SLO. The interviewer is checking whether you know that back-pressure belongs in the consumer, not in a bigger queue.

How do you stop a template bug from emailing 50,000 people the wrong thing?

Templates are versioned and the event pins the version it was rendered with, so a change never retroactively affects in-flight messages. Every broadcast above a threshold requires a canary: the first batch of 500 sends, then the pipeline pauses for a configured interval and continues only if the bounce rate is normal. And because notify.requested has seven days of retention, a bad send is diagnosable after the fact instead of being reconstructed from logs.

A user says they got the same alert three times. How do you investigate?

Query deliveries by event_id and employee. Three rows with the same event id means the dedupe key failed — usually because the producer generated a new event_id per attempt, which is the real bug. Three rows with different event ids means three genuine events, so the bug is upstream in whatever produced them. One row and three emails means the provider retried on its own. That triage order — ledger first, producer second, provider third — is the answer they want.

Why Kafka here rather than SQS or a database table?

Retention and replay. SQS deletes a message once it is consumed, so a bad render cannot be re-run; Kafka's log lets me fix the template and replay a day. Multiple independent consumer groups also come free, which is how the analytics pipeline reads the same stream without touching the notification path. If neither of those mattered, SQS with a DLQ would be less to operate and I would take it.

Languages and runtimes — Sheet 02
Why does adding threads not speed up a CPU-bound Python loop?

The Global Interpreter Lock lets only one thread execute Python bytecode at a time in CPython, so CPU-bound threads take turns rather than running in parallel. Threads still help I/O-bound work because the lock is released while waiting on a socket. For CPU work use multiprocessing, a library that releases the GIL such as NumPy, or move the work out of Python. Follow-up: “what about free-threaded builds?” — the GIL is becoming optional from 3.13, but do not claim production experience with it.

What does await actually do?

It suspends the current coroutine and returns control to the event loop, registering interest in a future. The loop runs other coroutines until the awaited thing is ready, then resumes yours at exactly that line with its stack intact. It is cooperative: nothing preempts you, so a blocking call between two awaits freezes every other request the process is serving.

Do TypeScript types exist at runtime?

No. tsc checks them and then erases them, emitting plain JavaScript. So anything arriving from outside — an HTTP response, JSON.parse, an environment variable — is unchecked no matter what type you annotated it with. Validate at the boundary with Zod and derive the type from the validator so there is one source of truth.

When would you use a generator instead of a list in Python?

When the sequence is large or unbounded and you consume it once. A list of a million badge events materialises every element in memory; a generator holds one at a time and composes lazily through a pipeline. The cost is that you cannot index it, cannot take its length, and it is exhausted after one pass — which is a real bug when someone iterates it twice.

What is the difference between ?? and || in JavaScript?

|| falls back on any falsy value, so 0, '' and false all trigger the default. ?? falls back only on null and undefined. Almost every config bug of the form “my limit of 0 became 10” is || where ?? was meant.

APIs and protocols — Sheet 05
400 versus 409 versus 422 versus 503 — when each?

400 the request is malformed and cannot be parsed. 422 the request parsed but failed a business rule — ends_on before starts_on. 409 the request is valid but conflicts with current state — this request was already approved. 503 we are unable to serve right now, retry with backoff; pair it with Retry-After. The distinction that matters to a client is whether retrying the identical request could ever succeed: for 400 and 422 never, for 409 only after state changes, for 503 probably.

How do you make a POST idempotent?

The client generates an Idempotency-Key, usually a UUID, and sends it as a header. The server stores key to result in Redis with a 24-hour TTL, writing the key inside the same transaction as the effect. A second call with the same key returns the stored response rather than doing the work again. The subtle part is a concurrent duplicate: the key must be inserted with a uniqueness guarantee so the loser waits or gets 409, not so both proceed.

When does GraphQL hurt you?

Three places. Caching — you lose HTTP and CDN caching because everything is a POST to one URL. Cost control — a client can write a deeply nested query that costs you the database, so you need depth limits, complexity scoring and persisted queries. And N+1 — naive resolvers issue one query per node, which needs DataLoader batching. GraphQL earns its keep when many different clients need different shapes of the same graph.

How do you version an API without breaking clients?

Additive changes need no version: new optional fields, new endpoints. For breaking changes use a URL version or a media-type version, run both for a deprecation window, and instrument usage per version so you know who is still on the old one. Never repurpose an existing field's meaning — that is a breaking change disguised as an additive one, and it will not show up in any test.

How do you verify an inbound webhook?

HMAC over the raw request body with a shared secret, compared in constant time, plus a timestamp header rejected outside a five-minute window to stop replays, plus an idempotency check on the event id because providers deliver more than once. Verify against the raw bytes — parsing then re-serialising changes the body and the signature will never match.

Databases — Sheet 06
What does an index cost you?

Storage, and write amplification: every insert, update or delete of an indexed column must maintain the B-tree, so a table with eight indexes writes nine times. It also gives the planner more choices, which is usually good and occasionally catastrophic. The rule is that an index must be paid for by a query you can name; unused indexes are pure cost and pg_stat_user_indexes will tell you which ones they are.

Index on (site_id, created_at) or (created_at, site_id)?

Leading column first by equality, then by range. The dashboard filters site_id = 'BLR-01' and orders by created_at DESC, so (site_id, created_at) lets the planner seek straight to that site and read rows already in order. The reverse forces a scan across all sites. Add WHERE status = 'pending' as a partial index and it becomes a fraction of the size and stays in memory.

What is the default isolation level in Postgres and what can still go wrong?

Read Committed. Each statement sees a fresh snapshot, so you get no dirty reads but you can get non-repeatable reads and phantom rows within a transaction. The practical consequence is the lost update: read a row, decide in application code, write it back, and a concurrent transaction can slip between the read and the write. Fix it with SELECT … FOR UPDATE, a unique constraint, or REPEATABLE READ with retry on serialisation failure.

When would you actually choose MongoDB over Postgres?

When documents genuinely vary in shape and you query them whole — our badge audit events differ per vendor and are always read as a document, never joined. That is a real fit. It is not a fit for anything with relationships you will need to enforce, and “for scale” is not a reason: a single Postgres will carry far more than most systems ever produce. If the variance is modest, a jsonb column removes a whole database from the diagram.

Your cache and your database disagree. How did that happen and what do you do?

Almost always a write that updated the database and then failed, or raced, on the cache delete. Prefer delete-on-write over update-on-write — deleting is idempotent, updating can write a stale value from a slow request. Put a TTL on everything so the worst case is bounded, and for hot keys add jitter to the TTL so ten thousand keys do not expire in the same second and stampede the database. If correctness cannot tolerate staleness, do not cache it.

Cloud — Sheet 07
What actually makes a subnet public?

A route table entry sending 0.0.0.0/0 to an internet gateway. Nothing else — not the name, not the CIDR. A private subnet reaches the internet outbound through a NAT gateway, which allows egress but no inbound connections. In BadgeDesk only the load balancer sits in the public subnets; the gateway, the core and all three databases are private.

Security group versus network ACL?

A security group is stateful and attaches to an instance or interface: allow inbound 443 and the response is automatically permitted. A NACL is stateless and attaches to a subnet, so you must allow the return traffic explicitly, and it supports deny rules, which security groups do not. Everyday work is security groups; NACLs are for coarse subnet-level blocks.

How does an application get database credentials without a secret in an env var?

An IAM role. On EC2 the instance profile, on EKS a service account mapped to a role through OIDC, and the SDK fetches short-lived credentials automatically and rotates them. For the database itself, either IAM authentication tokens for RDS or Secrets Manager with automatic rotation. The thing you are being tested on is that a long-lived secret pasted into an environment variable is the thing to eliminate, not to encrypt.

Lambda or a container — how do you choose, and what about cold starts?

Lambda for spiky, short, event-shaped work with no persistent connections — our nightly reconciler is a perfect fit. Containers for steady traffic, long-lived connections, or anything needing more than fifteen minutes. Cold starts matter for user-facing latency and barely at all for a nightly job; the fixes are provisioned concurrency, a smaller package, and avoiding heavyweight imports at module level. Cost inverts around the point where the function is warm most of the time.

Containers and delivery — Sheet 09
Image, container, layer — distinguish them.

An image is an immutable stack of read-only layers plus metadata. A layer is the filesystem diff produced by one build instruction, content-addressed and shared between images. A container is a running process using that image with a thin writable layer on top plus namespaces and cgroups for isolation. The practical consequence: ordering your Dockerfile so dependencies install before source is copied is what makes rebuilds fast.

Liveness or readiness — which one should check the database?

Readiness. Readiness failing takes the pod out of the load balancer; liveness failing kills and restarts it. If liveness checks the database, a brief database blip restarts every pod simultaneously, turning a small problem into an outage. Liveness should only detect a process that is genuinely wedged — deadlocked, out of threads — and should be the cheapest possible check.

Why a multi-stage Docker build?

To keep build-time tooling out of the runtime image. Compile or install with the full toolchain in one stage, copy only the artefact into a slim base in the final stage. That cuts the image from hundreds of megabytes to tens, removes compilers and package managers that are attack surface, and means your CVE scanner has far less to complain about. Add a non-root user in the final stage while you are there.

Rolling, blue/green or canary — and how does a rollback work?

Rolling replaces pods gradually and is the default; blue/green runs two full environments and flips traffic, giving an instant rollback at double the cost; canary sends a small percentage to the new version and watches metrics. Rollback in all three means routing back to the previous image, which only works if the image was built once and promoted rather than rebuilt per environment. The question behind the question is database migrations: they must be backwards-compatible, or the code rolls back and the schema cannot.

Messaging — Sheet 11
What guarantees ordering in Kafka?

Ordering exists per partition, not per topic. Messages with the same key hash to the same partition, so keying by employee_id keeps one employee's events ordered while allowing parallelism across employees. Global ordering requires a single partition, which caps throughput at one consumer. Choosing the key is choosing your ordering guarantee, and saying that sentence is the whole answer.

What happens to in-flight messages during a consumer group rebalance?

Partitions are revoked and reassigned, so work in progress on a revoked partition is abandoned and whoever gets it starts from the last committed offset — which is why anything processed but not committed is redelivered. If you commit before processing you lose messages; commit after and you get duplicates, which is the correct trade because your consumer is idempotent. Long processing times also trigger rebalances by blowing max.poll.interval.ms.

At-least-once delivery — what does it force you to do?

Make every consumer idempotent, because duplicates are guaranteed rather than exceptional. In practice that means a natural key with a unique constraint, an upsert, or a dedupe key in Redis with a TTL longer than any retry chain. It also means the DLQ is part of the design and needs an owner and an alarm — a dead-letter topic nobody looks at is just a slower way to lose data.

Kafka or RabbitMQ — one sentence each.

Kafka is a durable, replayable, partitioned log: pick it when multiple independent consumers need the same stream, when you need to replay history, or when throughput is high. RabbitMQ is a broker with rich routing and per-message acknowledgement: pick it for task queues, complex routing topologies and per-message TTLs and priorities. The tell is replay — if you would ever want to re-read yesterday, you want a log.

AI engineering — Sheet 12
Why RAG rather than fine-tuning for “Ask BadgeDesk”?

Because the problem is knowledge, not behaviour. Access policies change weekly and must be citable; retrieval lets me update a document and have the answer change immediately, and lets me show the source. Fine-tuning changes style and format, costs a training run per update, and cannot cite anything. Fine-tune when you need a consistent output shape; retrieve when you need current facts.

What is a token, and why does chunking matter so much?

A token is a sub-word unit — roughly four characters of English — and it is what you pay for and what fills the context window. Chunking matters because retrieval quality dominates answer quality: chunks too large and the embedding blurs several topics together so similarity search returns noise; too small and you lose the context that makes the passage meaningful. Chunk on document structure with a small overlap, and keep the section heading in the chunk.

How do you know a prompt change made things better?

With an evaluation set, not a vibe. Fifty to a hundred real questions with expected answers or graded criteria, run before and after, scoring retrieval separately from generation — because most regressions are retrieval, not the model. Track refusal rate and citation accuracy too. If you cannot say “this change moved the score from 0.71 to 0.78 on our eval set”, you are guessing, and that is the honest answer to give when you have not built one yet.

“Tell me about a time you failed”

This question is not looking for humility and it is definitely not looking for “my greatest weakness is that I care too much”. It is checking three specific things: that you can see your own contribution clearly, that the failure was real enough to be worth learning from, and that something systemic changed afterwards. A good answer contains four parts, in this order.

Part 1A real failure

Something with a consequence a stranger would recognise as bad — downtime, a missed date, data that had to be repaired. Not “I once underestimated a ticket”.

Part 2Your part in it, named

Say the decision you made and why it seemed reasonable at the time. Not the team's part, not the process's part. Yours.

Part 3What you did in the moment

How you detected it, how you contained it, who you told and how fast. Escalating early is a strength; people who hide failures are unhireable at senior level.

Part 4The systemic change

What exists now that did not exist before — a check, an alarm, a rule, a migration policy. This is the part being scored, and the part most candidates omit.

A worked failure answer, in about ninety seconds

“I ran a migration that added a NOT NULL column with a default to the access_requests table during business hours. On the version of Postgres we were on, that rewrote the whole table and held an ACCESS EXCLUSIVE lock for about forty minutes, so every approval failed. I had tested it on a seeded local database with 400 rows, and production had eleven million — that was my mistake, and it was a mistake of judgement, not of knowledge: I knew about table rewrites and did not think to check the row count. I noticed within four minutes from the error-rate alarm, could not cancel safely once the rewrite had started, so I posted in the incident channel immediately with an honest estimate rather than waiting to see if it would finish. Afterwards I wrote the rule we still use: migrations run against a restored production-sized snapshot in CI, any statement taking a lock over five seconds fails the build, and column additions are split into add-nullable, backfill in batches, then add the constraint. That rule has caught three migrations since.”

Red pencil — the answer that blames a team-mate

“The project failed because the other team did not deliver their API on time” is a catastrophic answer even when it is completely true. The interviewer is not evaluating the other team; they cannot. They are evaluating what you do when other people let you down — and the answer you just gave is “I wait, and then I explain”. The senior version keeps every fact and changes the subject back to you: “the dependency slipped by three weeks. In hindsight I should have found that out in week one instead of week four — I had taken a status update at face value rather than asking to see it working. When I did find out, I built a recorded fake of their API so we could keep integrating, and I raised the risk with both managers the same day.” Same facts, no blame, and it demonstrates three competencies at once. The same rule applies to former managers and former employers: if you criticise them, the interviewer's only question is how you will describe them in two years.

Take-home tests

The stated budget is almost always optimistic and the marking is almost always about engineering judgement rather than features. Decide your own time box, state it in the README, and stop when it expires.

SpendFour hours, whatever they said

Diminishing returns start immediately after the core feature works, and a reviewer comparing twenty submissions is skimming for signals, not admiring completeness. Decline politely if the task is unpaid and honestly needs more than eight hours — and say why. That is data for them, and a company that reacts badly has told you something.

How to run a mock interview alone

Finding a partner for six mocks is hard; recording yourself is not. This is the single highest-return activity on this sheet and almost nobody does it, for the obvious reason.

the solo mock protocol — 45 minutes total
1. Pick a design question you have NOT prepared. Write it on a card.
2. Start a screen recording with the microphone on. Excalidraw open, blank.
3. Talk for exactly 20 minutes. No pausing, no restarting, no editing.
   Treat every silence as if an interviewer were watching it - because
   one will be, in about a week.
4. Stop. Take a five-minute break. Do not skip the break: you need to
   stop being the person who gave the answer.
5. Watch it back at 1x with a pen, and count only these five things:

     - seconds before you asked your first clarifying question
     - number of silences longer than 8 seconds
     - number of times you said "basically", "kind of", "sort of"
     - whether you ever said a NUMBER
     - whether you ever said "the trade-off here is"

6. Write one sentence: the single thing to change next time. One, not five.
7. Repeat twice more that week with different questions.
Why watching yourself back is worth more than a fourth mock partner

It is genuinely uncomfortable. Everyone hates their recorded voice, everyone discovers they say “basically” forty times, and most people want to stop after four minutes. Do it anyway, because it is the only way to see the gap between the answer you think you gave and the one that was actually audible. Almost every candidate discovers the same three things: they started drawing before asking anything, they never said a single number, and there was a twenty-second silence they have no memory of. You cannot get that feedback from a friend, who will be too kind, and you will not get it from an interviewer, who will simply decline you. Three recordings watched back are worth more than ten practice questions.

Drill book — the mixed set

Eight questions that cut across rounds, in the exact words an interviewer would use. Answer each in under ninety seconds, out loud, standing up. These are the ones that separate a strong hire from a pass.

Cross-cutting — ask a partner to pick four at random
Walk me through what happens when a user clicks “Request Access”.

Narrate Drawing 14-E: browser to gateway, where the token is validated and the token bucket is checked; gateway to core over HTTP inside the VPC; the core evaluates site policy against a Redis-cached copy, writes one row in a transaction, publishes to badge.requests keyed by employee, and returns 202 Accepted with a status URL in about 40 ms. The provisioning worker consumes, calls the vendor with a five-second timeout and the request id as the idempotency key, and writes the badge under a unique constraint. Three retries with jittered backoff, then the dead-letter topic with an alarm. Nightly, a Lambda reconciles both sides into S3. Then stop and let them choose.

Your API's p99 latency doubled overnight. Nothing was deployed. What do you do?

First establish where: p99 at the load balancer, the application and the database tell you which hop grew. Nothing deployed means something else changed — data volume crossing a point where the planner flipped a plan, a cache that stopped being warm, a dependency that got slower, or a noisy neighbour. I would check slow-query logs and connection-pool saturation first, because pool exhaustion is the classic “p99 doubled, p50 fine” signature. Then dependency latency, then GC or CPU throttling. The habit being tested is measuring before theorising.

How would you design BadgeDesk differently if you started again tomorrow?

Two things. I would not split the gateway from the core at that traffic — one deployable would have saved a lot of cross-service debugging for no real benefit, and the only split that earned its keep was the provisioning worker. And I would put the reconciler in from day one rather than six months in, because we spent those six months not knowing about drift that was already happening. Being able to criticise your own design specifically is a stronger signal than defending it.

You disagree with your tech lead about a design. They have decided. What do you do?

Make the argument once, properly and in writing, with the specific consequence I am worried about and what evidence would change my mind. If the decision still goes the other way, I commit fully — including telling other people it is the decision, not undermining it in standups — and I write down the assumption we are betting on and what signal would prove it wrong, so that if it does go wrong we notice early rather than argue about who said what. Disagree and commit, with an instrumented tripwire.

What is the most interesting bug you have debugged?

Pick one with a surprising cause and a clean narrative: symptom, first wrong theory, what made you abandon it, the actual mechanism, and the systemic fix. The interval off-by-one from the coding section is a good one — a merge that used < instead of <=, invisible in tests, surfaced months later as a discrepancy in a nightly audit report. Interesting means “the cause was not where the symptom was”, not “it was hard”.

How do you decide when a piece of code needs a test?

When it encodes a decision rather than plumbing. Business rules, boundary arithmetic, anything with an if that a regulator would care about, and every bug you have already fixed once. I do not test framework wiring or getters, and I would rather have six tests that fail for six different real reasons than four hundred that all break when a constructor changes. The follow-up is coverage percentage — say that coverage is a smoke detector, not a goal.

You have one week and can improve one thing about a system you inherited. How do you choose?

By what is currently costing the most, measured rather than felt: on-call pages in the last month, the slowest thing in the deployment loop, and where people say “careful, do not touch that”. Usually the answer is not the architecture, it is observability — one week spent making failure visible pays for itself every week afterwards, and it also tells you what the second week should be. Answering with a diagnostic process rather than a favourite technology is the point.

Why do you want to leave your current role?

Answer in terms of what you are moving towards, with one concrete thing your current role structurally cannot give you — ownership of a service end to end, distributed systems at a scale that does not exist there, more time in design than in maintenance. Keep it under thirty seconds and never criticise your employer or a named person; the interviewer immediately imagines you describing them the same way. If the honest reason is compensation, say “scope and growth, and yes, compensation is part of it” — that is credible; pretending otherwise is not.
